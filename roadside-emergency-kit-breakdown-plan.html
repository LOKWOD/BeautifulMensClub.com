Roadside Emergency Kit and Breakdown Plan: Vehicle, Visibility and Seasonal Checks

A bin of gadgets is not a breakdown plan. Match supplies to the exact vehicle and people aboard, decide how you will call and be found, and rehearse the decisions that matter before traffic, weather or darkness raises the stakes.

Published October 6, 2026. General preparedness and consumer guidance, not emergency-response, vehicle-repair, towing, medical or legal advice. Follow local law, the vehicle manual and instructions from emergency personnel. Call 911 for immediate danger, injury, fire, smoke, fuel or chemical release, downed power lines, flooding, or a vehicle exposed to fast traffic when you cannot reach safety.

The useful answer

  • Build from vehicle, route, climate, occupants and communication coverage; a universal prepacked kit cannot account for all five.
  • Decide the safe breakdown sequence in advance: control the vehicle if possible, communicate exact location, improve visibility only when safe and follow official instructions.
  • Use only vehicle-compatible tools and maker-approved procedures; hybrids and electric vehicles add high-voltage hazards that belong with trained responders.
  • Inspect the kit every season and before long trips, rotate food, water, medicines and batteries, and check the exact vehicle for open safety recalls.

Profile the vehicle, route and people first

Record the year, make, model, powertrain, tire arrangement, spare or repair system, jack points, tow points, wheel-lock key, charging or fuel needs and the location of the owner’s manual. Then describe the usual routes: urban highway, rural road, mountain pass, heat, cold, flood-prone crossings, poor cellular coverage or long distances between services. The kit for a short city commute is not automatically adequate for a winter road trip, and extra equipment is not useful when nobody knows its limits.

Add the people and animals who normally ride. Note mobility or communication needs, child restraints, pet control, essential medication planning, temperature vulnerability and the amount of water that can be stored and rotated safely. Keep sensitive medical or identity information limited and secured. Tell a trusted person the route and expected arrival when the trip warrants it. The plan should work for the least independent occupant, not only the driver.

  • Vehicle: manual, powertrain, tire plan, recovery points and roadside-assistance terms.
  • Route: traffic exposure, weather, isolation, coverage, construction and known alternate stops.
  • Occupants: warmth, water, food, medicines, accessibility, children and pets.
  • Contact: who receives the route, when they should check in and which authority handles the road.

Write the first five breakdown decisions

If the vehicle develops a problem, maintain control, signal and move out of active travel lanes only when it can be done safely and lawfully. Stop in the safest available place, set the brake, follow the vehicle manual and assess traffic, fire, smoke, leaking fluid, water, unstable ground and electrical hazards before opening a door. Whether occupants should remain belted inside or move elsewhere depends on the exact location and official direction; there is no universal shoulder rule that defeats fast traffic or another immediate hazard.

Call 911 for immediate danger or injury. Otherwise contact the road authority, roadside provider or tow service and state the road name or number, direction of travel, nearest mile marker or exit, vehicle description, problem, occupant needs and callback number. Use phone location tools as a supplement, not the only description. Do not stand between vehicles, enter moving lanes, touch downed wires, walk through floodwater or crawl under a vehicle supported only by a jack.

  • Control: reduce risk and move out of the travel path only when safely possible.
  • Assess: traffic, fire, smoke, fluid, water, weather, ground and occupant condition.
  • Call: emergency services for danger; otherwise the correct road or roadside provider.
  • Describe: exact location, direction, vehicle, hazard, people, needs and callback number.

Build supplies by function, not by kit count

NHTSA includes a charged phone and charger, first-aid kit, flashlight, jumper cables, water and nonperishable food among roadside-kit considerations. Ready.gov adds general emergency-supply planning. Translate those categories to the vehicle: a compatible charging cable and backup power plan, reflective or lighted visibility equipment permitted where you drive, weather layers, work gloves, drinking water, shelf-stable food, first-aid supplies and a paper location or contact backup. Secure heavy items so they do not become projectiles.

Do not assume a prepacked kit is federally certified for every use. NHTSA has stated in an interpretation that it does not have a federal motor-vehicle safety standard specifically for emergency road kits. Inspect every component, instructions, battery, expiration and compatibility yourself. A multipurpose tool is not a substitute for the correct wheel hardware, a generic inflator may not solve sidewall damage, and jumper equipment must match the vehicle manual and powertrain.

  • Communication: charged phone, vehicle-compatible cable, backup power and paper contact/location notes.
  • Visibility: flashlight, spare power and lawful warning devices that can be deployed without entering danger.
  • People: first aid, water, food, weather protection, accessibility items and pet supplies.
  • Vehicle: only exact-fit, manual-approved tools and consumables you understand how to use safely.

Respect high voltage, fuel, heat and water

For a hybrid or electric vehicle, read the manufacturer’s emergency and towing instructions and keep them accessible. Orange high-voltage components, a damaged traction battery, smoke, heat, hissing, unusual odor or submerged electrical equipment are responder hazards, not do-it-yourself troubleshooting. Tell dispatch and the tow operator the exact powertrain and damage. Use only the approved charge and jump-start procedures; the presence of a familiar 12-volt battery does not authorize a generic sequence.

For any powertrain, do not smoke or create a spark near fuel or vapors, open a hot cooling system, or work beneath a vehicle supported only by a jack. Never drive or walk into floodwater; water depth, current, road damage and electrical hazards can be hidden. In heat or cold, shelter decisions depend on the vehicle condition, ventilation, fuel or charge, traffic location and official advice. Carbon monoxide is invisible, so never run an engine in an enclosed space or where the exhaust is blocked.

  • High voltage: identify the vehicle to responders and do not touch damaged or submerged components.
  • Fuel or smoke: move away when safe, keep ignition sources clear and call emergency services.
  • Flooding: turn around before entering water and follow closures and evacuation directions.
  • Extreme temperature: carry seasonal protection and use official local instructions for shelter and rescue.

Create a glove-box record and a phone backup

Keep a concise record with vehicle identity, insurer and policy contact route, roadside membership, preferred tow destination, emergency contacts, relevant accommodation needs, the vehicle maker’s roadside and towing instructions, and the current kit inventory. Do not leave unnecessary identity documents, account passwords or detailed medical history in an unattended vehicle. A photo of the current registration and VIN can help identify the vehicle, but store digital records behind the phone’s security controls and follow local document requirements.

Add a location script: road, direction, mile marker or exit, nearby landmark, vehicle color and plate, hazard and number of occupants. Download offline maps for remote trips when appropriate, but keep a paper route backup. A charged phone does not guarantee coverage or dispatch accuracy. Practice finding the hazard switch, manual, kit and roadside number in the dark without stepping into traffic.

  • Vehicle card: exact model, VIN, powertrain, tire plan, tow instructions and recall-check date.
  • Service card: insurer, roadside plan, limits, tow destination and local road authority.
  • Location script: road, direction, marker, landmark, vehicle, hazard and people.
  • Privacy check: carry only the information the task requires and secure the rest.

Run a seasonal and pre-trip inspection

Every three months and before a significant trip, unpack the kit. Charge and test power banks and lights, replace leaking batteries, rotate water and food by their labeled conditions, verify medicines with a pharmacist or prescriber when relevant, inspect first-aid contents, unfold weather gear and confirm that every occupant still fits the plan. Heat and freezing can degrade stored products, so follow each maker’s storage limits rather than leaving sensitive supplies in the vehicle indefinitely.

Check tires, lights, wipers, fluids and required service under the owner’s manual, and use NHTSA’s VIN lookup or SaferCar resources for open recalls. Confirm roadside coverage, route weather, closures, fuel or charging stops and the mobile network. Record the inspection date, replacement dates, missing items and who completed it. After any incident, replenish what was used and write one change that would make the next response clearer.

  • Quarterly: power, batteries, food, water, first aid, clothing, seals, corrosion and secure storage.
  • Before travel: route, weather, closures, fuel or charge, tires, recall status and contact coverage.
  • After use: replace supplies, clean equipment and record what failed or was hard to reach.
  • Best kit: the smallest complete, maintained system matched to the actual vehicle and trip.

Frequently asked questions

What should be in a roadside emergency kit?

Build by function: communication, visibility, first aid, water and food, weather protection, occupant needs and exact vehicle-compatible equipment. NHTSA and Ready.gov provide official starting lists, but the vehicle, route and season determine the final kit.

Should I stay in the car after a breakdown?

It depends on traffic, fire, flooding, location and official direction. Move out of travel lanes only when safely possible, assess hazards, keep occupants belted when appropriate and call 911 when there is immediate danger.

Are prepacked roadside kits federally certified?

Do not assume so. NHTSA has explained that there is no federal motor-vehicle safety standard specifically for emergency road kits. Verify every component, claim, instruction and vehicle fit.

How often should a car emergency kit be checked?

Inspect it at least seasonally and before significant trips, and immediately after use. Rotate batteries, food, water, medicines and temperature-sensitive supplies according to their exact labels and conditions.
