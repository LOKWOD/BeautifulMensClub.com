Refrigerator and Freezer Food Safety Plan: Temperature, Outages and Records

Cold storage becomes safer when temperature, time and door openings are recorded before a problem—not reconstructed from memory after the lights come back on.

Published October 3, 2026. General food-safety education, not a substitute for current FDA, USDA, FoodSafety.gov, local public-health or product-specific instructions. When safety is uncertain, use the official chart or discard the food; never taste food to decide whether it is safe.

The useful answer

  • Keep the refrigerator at or below 40°F (4°C) and the freezer at 0°F (-18°C), using visible appliance thermometers and a simple weekly record.
  • Store raw animal foods contained below ready-to-eat foods, leave room for cold-air circulation and clean spills promptly.
  • During an outage, keep doors closed, record start and restoration times and use the current official food-by-food chart rather than smell or taste.
  • Document appliance temperatures, food categories and discard or refreeze decisions so another household member can audit the handoff.

Set a measurable cold-storage baseline

FDA guidance says to keep the refrigerator at or below 40°F (4°C) and the freezer at 0°F (-18°C). Put a refrigerator/freezer appliance thermometer where it can be read without unloading shelves, then record both readings at the same time each week. The displayed panel on an appliance can be useful, but an independent thermometer shows the temperature where food actually sits and remains available during a power interruption.

Create one card with appliance make and model, thermometer locations, normal readings, control setting, manual link, purchase or service date and the household contact responsible for follow-up. A single warm reading is a prompt to investigate, not permission to turn the control blindly. Check door closure, loading, vents and the manual; persistent warming, unusual noise, water or repeated cycling calls for qualified service.

  • Refrigerator target: 40°F (4°C) or below, with room for air to circulate.
  • Freezer target: 0°F (-18°C), while recognizing that quality still declines over time.
  • Record thermometer reading, date, time and any recent loading or long door opening.
  • Keep the manufacturer manual and service route with the record, not only inside a phone app.

Map shelves by risk and use

Give ready-to-eat food, produce, dairy, leftovers and raw animal foods deliberate locations. Contain raw meat, poultry and seafood so leaking packages cannot drip onto ready-to-eat food, and keep them below those foods. Store eggs in their carton in the refrigerator rather than the warmer door, as FDA advises. Do not pack shelves so tightly that cold air cannot move around the food.

Label leftovers and prepared food with the item and storage date, then follow current official storage charts and package instructions. A quality date is not automatically a food-safety deadline, and a good smell is not proof of safety. FDA notes that foodborne pathogens may be present even when food does not look, smell or taste spoiled. When an item or its history is uncertain, use the more conservative official guidance.

  • Top and ready-to-eat zone: cooked food and food that will not receive another kill step.
  • Contained raw zone: leakproof tray below ready-to-eat items, separated by animal-food type when needed.
  • Door zone: condiments and items suited to greater temperature variation, not default storage for eggs or milk.
  • Label zone: marker, tape and a visible list of foods that should be used first.

Prepare the outage record before an outage

Place the power-company number, appliance thermometer readings, a paper clock record and the FoodSafety.gov outage chart where the household can find them. FDA states that an unopened refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours and a full unopened freezer for about 48 hours. A half-full freezer is commonly given a shorter window in federal guidance. These are planning estimates, not a substitute for the temperature and condition of the food.

When power fails, write the start time and baseline temperatures, then keep refrigerator and freezer doors closed as much as possible. Log any unavoidable opening and what was removed. Do not crowd around the appliance repeatedly to check it. If dry ice, block ice or another contingency is considered, follow official instructions and supplier safety directions; dry ice introduces ventilation and cold-burn hazards and should not be improvised in a closed occupied space.

  • Outage start: date, time, refrigerator reading, freezer reading and whether each door was closed.
  • Door log: time, appliance, reason and duration for every necessary opening.
  • Utility log: outage notice, estimated restoration and actual restoration time.
  • Cooling-aid log: type, amount, placement, source instructions and person who handled it.

Make the restoration decision by food category

When power returns, record the time before opening the doors and read the thermometers. For freezer food, FDA says that food that still contains ice crystals or is 40°F or below may be refrozen or cooked, though quality may suffer. For refrigerated perishables, time above 40°F and the exact food category matter. Use the current FoodSafety.gov chart item by item rather than applying one answer to the entire refrigerator.

Never taste a food to test its safety, and do not rely on appearance or odor. Create three groups: keep, cook or refreeze under official guidance, and discard. Photograph thermometer readings if that helps the household record, then write the basis for each decision. When a person is pregnant, older, very young or immunocompromised, or when the time-temperature history cannot be established, contact a qualified clinician or local public-health authority for situation-specific guidance and use the conservative disposal choice.

  • Record the exact food, package condition, measured temperature, ice crystals and time history.
  • Use the federal food-by-food outage chart; do not group unlike foods by shelf location alone.
  • Mark every disposal decision so a questionable item is not returned to storage by someone else.
  • If a product is under recall, follow the recall notice even when temperature control was maintained.

Clean without spreading the problem

Clean spills promptly, especially those from raw animal foods. Move food to safe cold storage if a longer cleaning session is needed, and follow the appliance manual for shelf removal and approved cleaners. Use separate cleaning materials where cross-contamination is a concern. Do not mix cleaning chemicals, and ventilate as directed on labels. A fresh smell is not evidence that surfaces or food are safe.

After an outage, leak or spoiled-food event, document the affected shelf, items, cleaning product, contact time if specified, rinse step if required and date. Inspect door gaskets and drain areas only as the manual permits. If floodwater, sewage, chemical contamination, broken glass or pest contamination reaches food or food-contact surfaces, stop the ordinary cleaning routine and use current public-health or emergency guidance.

  • Remove and contain the source before wiping surrounding surfaces.
  • Clean from less contaminated areas toward the affected area with materials that will not spread residue.
  • Follow product label dilution, contact, rinse, glove and ventilation directions exactly.
  • Return food only after the compartment is clean, dry and back at a safe measured temperature.

Use a one-page weekly and incident handoff

The weekly record needs only date, refrigerator temperature, freezer temperature, unusual loading or door problem, corrective action and initials. Add a short use-first list and a note for any raw package that needs a better tray. This small routine catches drift, keeps the thermometer visible and makes the next grocery load easier to place without crowding vents.

The incident record adds outage start and end, door openings, cooling aids, restoration readings, food-by-food decisions, disposal, cleaning and any service or public-health contact. Keep it with receipts and photographs when a failed appliance or utility claim may be involved. Review the event afterward: move the thermometer, print a newer chart, replace an unreadable marker or change the emergency plan while the evidence is still clear.

  • Weekly line: date, two temperatures, door seal check, spill check and use-first items.
  • Incident line: trigger, time-temperature record, decisions, authority consulted and responsible person.
  • Service line: symptom, model and serial, technician, diagnosis, parts and restored-temperature check.
  • Review line: what failed in the plan and the exact change made before the next incident.

Frequently asked questions

What temperature should a refrigerator and freezer be?

FDA guidance says a refrigerator should be at or below 40°F (4°C) and a freezer should be 0°F (-18°C). Use visible appliance thermometers and investigate persistent drift.

How long is refrigerator food safe during a power outage?

FDA says an unopened refrigerator keeps food cold for about four hours. After that, measured temperature, time and the exact food category determine the decision; use the current FoodSafety.gov chart.

Can frozen food be refrozen after an outage?

FDA says food that still contains ice crystals or is 40°F or below may be refrozen or cooked, although quality can decline. Record the evidence and follow the exact official chart.

Can smell or taste show whether food is safe?

No. Pathogens may not change appearance, smell or taste, and tasting questionable food can expose you. Use time, temperature, category and official guidance; discard when the history is uncertain.
