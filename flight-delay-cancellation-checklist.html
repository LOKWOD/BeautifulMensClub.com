Flight Delay and Cancellation Checklist: Rebooking, Refunds and Receipts

The first useful move in a disrupted trip is not a dramatic dash to the service desk. Preserve the original itinerary, learn what the airline is offering, protect any onward segments and make the rebook-or-refund decision with timestamps and receipts.

Published October 8, 2026. Current as of October 8, 2026 and focused on flights to, from or within the United States. General travel organization, not legal, insurance or financial advice. Airline contracts, DOT enforcement, international passenger rights, credit-card benefits and insurance terms differ and can change; check the airline, U.S. Department of Transportation and any governing foreign authority for the exact trip.

The useful answer

  • Capture the original itinerary, ticket number, operating carrier, disruption notice, stated cause, boarding pass and baggage record before an app updates the trip.
  • Protect the full journey: ask whether onward and return segments remain confirmed before accepting, cancelling or failing to board a substitute.
  • For a cancellation or significant change, compare the offered alternative with the DOT refund right that applies when you choose not to accept the alternative; do not accept a voucher by reflex.
  • Keep itemized receipts and written promises, then request any airline commitment, insurance benefit or legal remedy through the correct channel without describing every delay as guaranteed compensation.

Freeze the original record before changing anything

Screenshot the booked itinerary, fare and cabin, ticket number, confirmation code, operating and marketing carriers, scheduled times, seat, boarding pass, baggage tag and any special-service request. Save the email, text, app notice and airport display showing the delay or cancellation, with timestamps. If the airline states a cause, record its exact words without rewriting weather, crew, maintenance or air-traffic control into a legal conclusion.

List every segment on the same ticket and every separate booking that depends on arrival: rail, hotel, rental car, event and return flight. A change to one segment can affect the rest. Do not delete the trip from an app or abandon a segment without asking what happens to onward and return travel. For checked baggage, record where the carrier says the bag will be held, transferred or delivered and the claim process for necessary interim purchases.

  • Trip identity: passenger name, ticket number, confirmation, carrier and every segment.
  • Disruption proof: original schedule, notice, airport display, stated cause and timestamp.
  • Dependencies: connections, separate tickets, hotel, ground transport and fixed events.
  • Baggage: tag, current location, delivery promise and written expense instructions.

Use parallel information channels without creating duplicate changes

Open the airline app or website, join the official phone or chat queue and, when useful, line up at the service desk. Check the operating carrier when a ticket was sold by a partner. One channel may surface a workable rebooking while another is still waiting, but confirm only one change and close the other sessions afterward. Avoid unofficial phone numbers from ads or social replies; navigate from the airline’s known app or domain.

Search reasonable alternatives before reaching an agent: same carrier later that day, a nearby airport, a connection instead of nonstop or a date change. Record the exact flight numbers and whether seats are actually offered. Do not purchase a costly replacement assuming reimbursement unless the airline, insurer or card benefit has authorized it in writing or you consciously accept the risk. A visible seat on another airline is not proof that the original carrier must buy it.

  • Official app or site: inspect self-service alternatives and preserve the offer before confirming.
  • Agent queue: state the desired flight number and protect all remaining segments.
  • Airport desk: use when physical documents, baggage or same-day operations require it.
  • Fraud gate: never give payment or login codes to an unverified account offering rescue.

Separate a refund right from an airline service commitment

DOT states that when an airline cancels a flight or makes a significant change, the passenger is entitled to a prompt refund if the passenger chooses not to accept the alternative transportation or other offered compensation. The refund rule is separate from meals, hotels, ground transport, travel credits and rebooking on another airline. Those added commitments on DOT’s Airline Cancellation and Delay Dashboard apply to controllable disruptions and vary by carrier.

Open the current DOT dashboard for the specific operating airline and commitment, then read the airline’s contract and disruption page. Ask the agent to state whether the event is considered controllable and to identify the written benefit being offered. Weather and other events outside airline control often receive different assistance. Do not surrender a refund choice for a voucher until you understand expiration, transferability, blackout, residual-value and acceptance terms.

  • Refund question: was the flight cancelled or significantly changed, and are you declining the alternative?
  • Commitment question: what does this carrier promise for this controllable disruption?
  • Contract question: what do the ticket terms say about rerouting, segments and deadlines?
  • Voucher question: what rights or cash refund are you giving up by accepting it?

Choose rebooking or refund as a whole-trip decision

For rebooking, compare arrival time, airport, number of stops, cabin, seat, baggage transfer and the chance of making the new connection. Ask for the complete confirmed itinerary in writing and verify every remaining segment in the app. If the alternate airport creates ground-transport cost or strands a rental car elsewhere, include that in the decision. A faster departure can still be a worse recovery when it breaks the rest of the trip.

For a refund, confirm the form of payment, covered ticket and optional-service amounts, and what happens to unused onward and return segments. Save the request date, channel, case number and response. DOT publishes timing and method rules, but exact application depends on the transaction. If part of the trip remains useful, ask before cancelling the entire reservation; do not assume an app button preserves the segments you want.

  • Rebook: confirm the full itinerary, not only the first substitute flight.
  • Refund: identify every unused segment and optional service included in the request.
  • Separate tickets: contact each carrier or provider before a no-show cancels later travel.
  • Decision record: note what was offered, what you accepted and the time and agent or channel.

Control meals, lodging, baggage and accessibility needs

Ask for any meal, hotel and ground-transport assistance before spending when practical, and save the written eligibility and limits. Keep itemized receipts showing merchant, date, items and amount; a card total alone may be insufficient. Choose reasonable necessities rather than assuming every expense is covered. Record any airline voucher separately and inspect where, when and with whom it can be used.

Tell the carrier promptly about wheelchair assistance, medication timing, service animals, unaccompanied-minor arrangements or another disability-related need affected by the change. DOT’s disability rules and airline procedures have dedicated channels; do not reduce them to an ordinary customer-service preference. Keep essential medication and documents with you as permitted, and ask qualified medical help when a delay creates a health concern. For baggage, use the carrier’s official claim process and deadlines.

  • Meals and hotel: get written instructions and limits, then retain itemized receipts.
  • Ground transport: document why it was necessary and whether the airline authorized it.
  • Accessibility: restate the exact assistance needed on the new itinerary and verify it.
  • Baggage: preserve tag, report number, delivery details and receipts for permitted necessities.

Close the case with one evidence packet

After travel, create a timeline with scheduled and actual events, notices, stated causes, contacts, offers, acceptance and expenses. Add the original ticket, revised itinerary, boarding passes, baggage record, receipts and screenshots of the relevant airline commitment as it appeared on the disruption date. Submit a concise request to the responsible airline first, asking for a named remedy and citing the carrier’s own commitment or the DOT refund rule accurately.

If the response does not address the issue, use DOT’s official consumer complaint route for U.S.-covered air travel and retain the complaint number. File insurance or card-benefit claims separately under their deadlines and evidence rules; double recovery is not a strategy. International legs may trigger different legal regimes, so use the governing authority or qualified counsel when stakes justify it. This dated checklist is a records system, not a promise that every disrupted traveler receives cash compensation.

  • Timeline: scheduled, changed and actual events with time zones and source.
  • Evidence: tickets, notices, offers, communications, receipts and commitment snapshots.
  • Request: one responsible carrier, one clear remedy and one accurate basis.
  • Escalation: DOT complaint, insurer, card benefit, foreign authority or qualified legal help as applicable.

Frequently asked questions

Does a U.S. airline have to pay cash compensation for every delay?

No. U.S. rules do not create universal cash compensation for every delay. Refund rights, carrier commitments for controllable disruptions, insurance and foreign-law remedies are different questions.

When can I get a refund instead of rebooking?

DOT says a refund is due when the airline cancels or makes a significant change and you choose not to accept the alternative transportation or other offered compensation. Check the current rule for the exact itinerary.

Will an airline pay for a hotel after a cancellation?

It depends on the carrier, stated cause and current commitment. Check DOT’s dashboard and the airline’s written policy; obtain authorization and limits before spending when practical.

Should I buy a replacement ticket on another airline?

Only after comparing the original carrier’s options and understanding who, if anyone, has agreed to reimburse it. A replacement can preserve the trip, but reimbursement is not automatic.
