Personal Trainer Consultation and Contract Checklist: Credential, Scope, Trial and Terms

A certificate logo, dramatic transformation photo or hard first workout does not prove a good fit. Verify the person, credential and scope; watch one paid session; then put the service, records, payment and exit route in writing.

Published November 23, 2026. General consumer and fitness guidance, not medical advice or a substitute for clinical care. A personal trainer should work within the credential, training and legal scope that applies. Use a qualified health professional for diagnosis, treatment, rehabilitation or individualized medical clearance.

The useful answer

  • Verify the individual trainer's current credential and the credential program's accreditation; a gym logo or organization membership is not the same evidence.
  • Define the goal, current activity, constraints and clinician-set limits before evaluating exercises or package size.
  • Ask how the trainer screens, programs, records, progresses, substitutes and refers—then observe those behaviors in a paid trial session.
  • Keep medical diagnosis, nutrition treatment, injury rehabilitation and promises of cure outside an ordinary personal trainer's lane unless separate lawful qualifications are verified.
  • Read price, package expiration, cancellation, refund, trainer substitution, privacy and facility-access terms before paying for multiple sessions.

Write the job before interviewing the person

Describe the outcome in behavior and performance terms: learn safe machine setup, build a repeatable two-day strength routine, improve exercise confidence, prepare for a specific nonmedical fitness test or stay accountable to scheduled sessions. Add the available days, session length, facility, travel tolerance and realistic budget. Avoid an outcome such as fix my back or balance my hormones; that wording asks a trainer to diagnose or treat a condition outside ordinary fitness coaching.

List current activity, previous training, equipment access, known movement constraints, medicines or health information you choose to share, and any instructions from a qualified clinician. MedlinePlus recommends working with a health professional when recovering from illness or injury, and warns against a trainer who contradicts provider orders or gives medical treatment advice. The consultation should clarify the boundary before it sells intensity.

  • Specific coaching outcome: ________
  • Days, location and budget ceiling: ________
  • Clinician-set limits or referral questions: ________
  • What is explicitly outside the job: ________

Verify identity, credential and current standing

Record the trainer's full professional name, employer or business, direct contact, credential title, credential number when available, issuing organization and renewal date. Use the issuer's verification directory or contact route; do not accept a screenshot or initials alone. Ask what education, supervised experience and continuing education support the exact population and goal. CPR and first-aid status, professional liability coverage and facility authorization are also concrete questions identified in ACSM's trainer-selection guidance.

Then verify the credential program rather than assuming every certification word has equal meaning. NCCA accredits certification programs against credentialing standards; accreditation is evidence about the program's development and operation, not an endorsement of every trainer or a guarantee of fit. Keep separate fields for individual credential status, program accreditation, degree, specialty education and legally required license. Precise language prevents a weekend course, expired certificate and regulated clinical license from collapsing into one badge.

  • Trainer identity and employer: ________
  • Credential, issuer, number and renewal: ________
  • Program accreditation verified at source: ________
  • CPR/first aid, insurance and facility authorization: ________

Map scope and referral before the first set

Ask the trainer to explain what the credential permits, which clients it covers, and when the trainer refers out. A useful answer distinguishes general exercise instruction and program design from medical diagnosis, physical therapy, prescription changes, eating-disorder care and individualized treatment of disease or injury. A separate nutrition or clinical credential must be verified on its own terms; a personal-training credential does not silently add those authorities.

Give two realistic boundary scenarios. What happens if you report chest pressure, faintness, a new neurologic symptom, persistent joint pain or a clinician restriction? What happens if you ask for rehabilitation after surgery or a therapeutic diet? The right response is not a memorized liability phrase. It is a clear stop, documentation and referral route. If the trainer markets guaranteed pain relief, detoxification, spot reduction or rapid body transformation, record the claim and walk away.

  • Trainer's stated scope: ________
  • Separate specialty or license verified: ________
  • Stop-and-refer examples discussed: ________
  • Unsupported promise or claim: ________

Inspect the intake, screening and privacy route

Before training, the trainer should ask about goals, activity history, health factors relevant to exercise, symptoms, medicines when appropriate, previous injuries, current care and preferences. ACSM's consumer guidance describes pre-activity screening and informed consent as part of the process. Screening does not diagnose health or prove that every exercise is safe; it identifies questions, supports a starting plan and triggers referral when the trainer's scope ends.

Ask which records are collected, where they are stored, who can see them, how messages and progress photos are handled, and when records are deleted. A gym app, shared spreadsheet or trainer's phone may hold sensitive information even when it is not a regulated medical record. Choose the minimum necessary data. Do not send diagnoses, identification or body photographs through a channel until you understand its access and retention.

  • Intake and pre-activity screening used: ________
  • Informed-consent and emergency process: ________
  • Data collected, storage and access: ________
  • Photo, messaging and deletion choices: ________

Use a paid trial as an observation, not an audition

Pay for one consultation or trial rather than accepting pressure to buy a large package first. Observe whether the trainer asks before touching, explains the purpose of an exercise, demonstrates and watches, chooses a manageable starting dose, adjusts equipment, invites feedback and records the result. The trainer should be engaged with your movement rather than a phone, nearby conversation or a generic circuit. Soreness, sweat and exhaustion are not quality metrics.

Score the session immediately afterward. Could you restate the session goal and the next step? Were exercises selected for the stated outcome and current ability? Did the trainer explain what would cause a substitution or stop? Was the environment respectful and free of humiliation, sexualized commentary and body shaming? A technically knowledgeable coach can still be the wrong match if communication, privacy, schedule or professional boundaries fail.

  • Purpose and plan explained: 0 / 1 / 2
  • Technique observation and useful feedback: 0 / 1 / 2
  • Consent, respect and attention: 0 / 1 / 2
  • Clear next step without pressure: 0 / 1 / 2

Ask for the program logic and review cadence

Request a plain-language outline: weekly frequency, session roles, exercise patterns, starting sets and effort, progression rule, recovery plan, independent work and review date. The federal Physical Activity Guidelines provide population-level evidence, not an individual prescription; a trainer should translate goals and response into a plan without presenting one template as a federal requirement. The plan should show how the first weeks teach technique and collect response before aggressive load or volume.

Ask how attendance, loads, repetitions, effort, symptoms and substitutions are recorded and shared. A good log makes a trainer change explainable: increase when target work is completed with the agreed quality and response; hold when recovery or technique is uncertain; reduce or refer when warning signs appear. The client should be able to continue the routine, change trainers or speak with a clinician without losing every useful record behind an app or private shorthand.

  • Weekly structure and independent work: ________
  • Starting dose and progression rule: ________
  • Review date and decision signals: ________
  • Exportable record and ownership: ________

Read the contract as a service map

Write the exact service: session length, location, one-to-one or group status, named trainer, facility access, assessment, programming, messaging, records and any separately priced work. Confirm the full price, tax, membership requirement, package size, expiration, automatic renewal and accepted payment method. A per-session price is incomplete if entry fees, required memberships, software or periodic reassessments add cost.

Read cancellation, rescheduling, no-show, trainer-absence, substitution, refund, transfer, pause, medical hold and termination terms. Ask what happens if the trainer leaves the facility or the facility closes. Get every exception in writing before payment and keep the signed version, receipt and policy snapshot. If financing or recurring billing is involved, compare the total obligation and cancellation evidence route, not merely the weekly number.

  • Named service, trainer and location: ________
  • Total cost and all dependencies: ________
  • Expiration, renewal and cancellation: ________
  • Trainer departure, pause and refund route: ________

Close the first month with a keep-or-change review

At the agreed review date, compare attendance, technique confidence, completed work, recovery, symptoms and progress toward the defined goal. Do not demand a dramatic body change on an arbitrary timeline. Look for a coherent record, timely adjustments, professional boundaries and a plan you understand. If the service differs from the agreement, document examples and ask for a written correction with a date.

Continue when the relationship is safe, explainable and useful. Change the program when the dose or schedule does not fit. Change the trainer when communication, attention, scope or trust fails. Stop and use the contract route for harassment, falsified credentials, unauthorized billing or dangerous conduct, and contact the credential issuer, facility, payment provider or appropriate regulator when the facts support it. Seek qualified medical care for concerning symptoms or injury rather than asking the contract to solve a health problem.

  • Review date and evidence: ________
  • Keep / adjust / refer / end: ________
  • Written correction or cancellation record: ________
  • Records exported and next handoff: ________

Frequently asked questions

Does an NCCA-accredited certification guarantee a good trainer?

No. It supports the certification program's credentialing process. You still need to verify the individual's current credential, scope, experience, conduct and fit.

Should a trainer diagnose my pain?

No. A trainer can stop or modify general exercise within scope, but diagnosis, treatment and rehabilitation require the appropriate qualified health professional.

Is a free hard workout a useful trial?

Not by itself. A paid consultation or controlled trial is more useful when it shows screening, explanation, observation, consent, recordkeeping and an appropriate starting dose.

How many sessions should I buy first?

Buy only what you can evaluate and use under clear expiration, cancellation and refund terms. A large package should not substitute for a verified fit.
