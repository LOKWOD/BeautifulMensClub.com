Home Ladder Guide: Type, Height, Duty Rating, Setup and Inspection

The safest ladder decision happens before the first step: define the task, remove the hazards, choose equipment that reaches without improvisation, and stop when the setup is wrong.

Published September 22, 2026. General household safety information, not a substitute for manufacturer instructions, building codes, workplace rules or qualified trade judgment. OSHA sources cited here govern covered workplaces, not ordinary home use.

The useful answer

  • Write the task, work height, surface, doorway and electrical conditions before choosing a stepladder, extension ladder, platform ladder or another access method.
  • Use the exact ladder label and manual for maximum load, permitted configuration, setup angle, highest standing level and compatible accessories.
  • Count the user, clothing, tools and materials in the total load; a duty rating is a limit, not a quality score or target.
  • Inspect rails, feet, steps, locks, rope, hardware and contamination before every use, and check the exact model in the CPSC recall database.
  • Stop for leaning, improvised height, live electrical exposure, unstable footing, damaged parts, bad weather or a task requiring two hands and force.

Use the task-to-ladder worksheet

Write one task per line: change a filter, clean a gutter, reach an attic, paint a stairwell or trim a tree. Record the work location, required working height, floor or ground surface, nearby doors and traffic, weather, overhead lines or wiring, tools and material load, and whether the work requires side force. This turns buy a ladder into a defined access problem.

Ask whether a ladder is the right access method at all. Repetitive work, long-duration work, heavy handling, two-handed force, stair geometry or a large working area may call for a platform, scaffold or qualified professional. A ladder is not automatically safer because the task is brief or familiar. The plan should include a clean route down and a way to summon help.

Use the recorded conditions to screen types before brands. A self-supporting stepladder needs room to open and lock fully. A straight or extension ladder needs a sound upper support and correctly prepared base. A platform ladder changes the standing area but not the need to respect labels. An attic stair is installed equipment and must match its opening, structure and current recall status.

Choose height without standing on the warning zone

Required work height is not ladder length. Your reach depends on body dimensions, the permitted highest standing level and the ladder's geometry. Read the exact label and maker selection chart. Do not assume the top cap or top step is a standing surface. If the task still requires tiptoes or reaching beyond the side rails, the equipment is too short or the setup is wrong.

For access to an upper level, follow the manufacturer's projection, tie-off and transition instructions plus any applicable code or workplace rule. The point where you step off matters as much as the climb. An extension ladder also requires overlap between sections, so its usable reach is shorter than its total extended length. Never splice ladders or set one on boxes to recover missing height.

Measure storage and transport before purchase. Long ladders need secure carrying, a route without striking lights or wires, and appropriate vehicle restraint. Folding or multi-position ladders can solve storage problems, but every hinge and configuration adds a specific locking check. A compact shape does not make every advertised position suitable for every task.

Read the duty rating and material label

The maximum load includes the user, clothing, carried tools and materials. Estimate the complete load with margin and stay within the exact label. Do not treat a higher duty rating as permission to bounce, overreach or ignore damage. Ratings cannot compensate for an unstable surface or a ladder used in a configuration the maker prohibits.

Material affects electrical and handling decisions but does not erase hazards. Metal conducts electricity. Fiberglass is commonly selected around electrical work, yet dirt, moisture, damage and proximity to energized parts still require control. Do not perform electrical work beyond your qualification, and never let a material label substitute for de-energizing, clearance and professional judgment.

Check the label for standard references, model, manufacture information, permitted configurations and warnings, but do not infer certification from a retailer badge alone. Confirm claims with the manufacturer or the named standards body when they matter. Save a readable photograph of the labels because sunlight, abrasion and cleaning can make them harder to read over time.

Run the pre-climb inspection

Start at one foot and trace the ladder to the other: feet and pads, rails, rungs or steps, spreaders, locks, hinges, fasteners, rope, pulley, guides, end caps and platform. Look for bends, splits, corrosion, missing hardware, looseness, contamination and unauthorized repairs. Confirm every lock engages as designed. If a critical part is damaged or uncertain, tag the ladder out of use.

Remove mud, oil, paint, water and loose material using the maker's instructions. Shoes need clean slip-resistant soles. Do not drill, weld, tape, splice or replace structural parts with generic hardware. A ladder repair is not successful merely because it holds body weight once; use maker-authorized parts and service or retire the equipment.

Check recalls using the exact brand, model and identifying labels. On August 6, 2026, CPSC announced a U.S. recall involving about 1.77 million specified Louisville, Featherlite, Lite and Century attic stairway ladders because affected gas-strut bolts could break. That dated example does not implicate every attic ladder; it shows why exact-model records and current recall checks belong in the inspection.

Prepare the base, top and traffic zone

Place the ladder on a stable, level, clean surface that can support the load. Do not use loose bricks, lumber scraps or furniture as leveling devices. Keep feet away from floor openings, soft ground and edges. For outdoor work, reassess soil after rain and stop in wind, ice, lightning or other conditions outside the ladder maker's limits.

Open self-supporting ladders fully and lock spreaders. Never lean a closed stepladder unless the exact model is designed and labeled for that configuration. For a non-self-supporting ladder, use the maker's angle method and secure the base and top as directed. The NIOSH Ladder Safety app offers angle and selection tools, but the ladder's instructions remain the model-specific authority.

Control doors, people, pets and vehicles before climbing. Lock or guard doors that could strike the ladder; do not rely on a handwritten note where another person can open them. Establish a visible exclusion zone and stage materials so nothing must be fetched by leaning. If a helper is needed, agree on roles and communication before the climb.

Climb with three points and a quiet center of gravity

Face the ladder and maintain three points of contact while climbing. Use a belt, hoist line or another maker-approved method for tools so hands remain available. Keep belt-buckle area between the rails as a practical overreach check. Descend and reposition instead of stretching sideways, stepping to another surface or making the ladder walk.

Respect the highest standing level and every label. Do not sit or stand on a top cap that is not designed for it, climb the rear braces unless they are intended as steps, or allow more users than the product permits. Do not move, shift or extend an occupied ladder. The convenience saved is never worth the lost stability.

Stop when work force changes the setup. Pulling hard, using a high-torque tool, carrying a bulky object or reacting to a stuck fastener can move the center of gravity suddenly. If the task requires both hands without stable support, repeated side loading or attention away from balance, choose a different access system or a qualified contractor.

Treat electricity and outdoor work as separate gates

Locate overhead and concealed electrical hazards before moving or raising a ladder. Maintain required clearances and have circuits de-energized and verified by qualified people where appropriate. A ladder can contact a line during carrying, setup or descent, before anyone climbs it. Carry long ladders horizontally only where the route is fully checked and the maker permits the method.

Tree work combines height, cutting force, falling material and possible conductors. Do not place a ladder against a branch that may move or cut a supporting limb. Chainsaws and ladders are a high-consequence combination that generally calls for specialized training and equipment. Hire qualified help rather than converting a household ladder into an arborist system.

Gutter and roof-edge work adds fragile materials, slippery debris and a transition near a fall edge. Do not step from a ladder onto a roof merely because the eave is reachable. Follow applicable fall-protection, access and manufacturer rules. If safe access cannot be described before setup, the project is not ready to begin.

Keep a ladder record and retirement rule

Record brand, model, serial or date code, ladder type, length, duty rating, purchase date, label photographs and manual URL. Add inspection dates, damage, authorized repairs and recall checks. For an installed attic stair, keep the installation and hardware record with the home file so a later owner or contractor can identify it without guessing.

Store ladders dry, secured and protected from impact, unauthorized use and material degradation. Support long ladders so rails do not sag. Do not hang tools or heavy objects from rungs, and do not leave a ladder deployed as casual access for children or visitors. Follow maker guidance for ultraviolet, temperature and chemical exposure.

Retire a ladder when structural damage, missing labels, uncertain locks, incompatible parts or a recall notice cannot be resolved through the manufacturer. Destroy or mark discarded equipment so another person will not retrieve it for use, following local disposal rules. The useful life is determined by condition, history and maker criteria—not by a confidence-inspiring color or an invented anniversary.

Frequently asked questions

How tall a ladder do I need?

Use the maker's selection chart, permitted highest standing level and required work height. Ladder length and safe reach are not the same, and extension sections require overlap.

What does ladder duty rating include?

The total of the user, clothing, tools and materials. Stay within the exact label; a higher rating does not correct unstable setup or misuse.

Can I lean a stepladder against a wall?

Only if the exact model is designed and labeled for that use. A conventional stepladder is generally intended to be opened fully with spreaders locked.

When should I replace a ladder?

Remove it from use for structural damage, failed or uncertain locks, missing critical labels, unauthorized repair or an applicable unresolved recall. Follow the manufacturer for inspection and retirement criteria.
