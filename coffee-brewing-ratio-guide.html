Coffee Brewing Ratio Guide: Weigh, Grind, Time and Adjust

Good coffee becomes repeatable when the recipe is written in grams and time, then changed one variable at a time. The ratio is a starting point; the log is what teaches you where to go next.

Published October 3, 2026. Independent technique guide. Coffee preferences, beans, water, grinders and brewers vary; follow the exact brewer and grinder instructions. Hot water and steam can burn—use stable equipment and keep children and cords clear.

The useful answer

  • Write coffee dose and brew water in grams so the ratio can be scaled without changing the recipe's shape.
  • Start with a documented middle recipe—such as 1:16 for many filter methods—then use the brewer instructions and taste to adjust.
  • Hold dose, water and technique steady while changing grind or time; changing several variables at once hides the cause.
  • Log bean, roast date, grinder setting, water, temperature, contact time, beverage yield and a plain-language tasting result.

Write the ratio in grams

A coffee-to-water ratio states how many grams of brew water are used for each gram of coffee. At 1:16, 20 grams of coffee pairs with 320 grams of water; 30 grams pairs with 480 grams. This is a practical middle starting point for many filter brews, not a universal quality law. Espresso, cold brew concentrate, immersion brewers and manufacturer-designed pods use different relationships and should follow their own instructions.

Weighing makes a recipe scalable and avoids the ambiguity of scoops, bean size and heaped tablespoons. Put the empty brewing vessel on the scale, tare it, weigh the dry coffee, then tare or note the water separately as the method requires. Record whether the number is water poured, water in the reservoir or beverage in the cup; those are not interchangeable because coffee and equipment retain water.

  • 1:15 example: 20 g coffee and 300 g brew water.
  • 1:16 example: 20 g coffee and 320 g brew water.
  • 1:17 example: 20 g coffee and 340 g brew water.
  • Scale by multiplication, then round only to the precision your scale can repeat.

Choose a method-specific baseline

Start with the brewer maker's dose, water, filter and assembly instructions, then compare them with reputable coffee-industry guidance. The National Coffee Association emphasizes that brewing method and process materially affect the cup, and that drip, pour-over, espresso, French press and cold brew are distinct methods. A ratio that works in a flat-bottom filter does not automatically transfer to a small espresso basket or an overnight concentrate.

Write a complete baseline before tasting: bean name and roast date if supplied, dose, water, ratio, grinder and setting, water source, temperature if measured, pour or steep sequence, total contact time and beverage yield. Mark any deviation such as a stalled drawdown, channeling, torn filter or interrupted pour. Without that context, a tasting note becomes a verdict on the beans when the process may be the reason.

  • Automatic drip: record reservoir water, basket dose, filter type and completed brew time.
  • Pour-over: record dose, total water, bloom, pour sequence and drawdown time.
  • French press: record dose, water, grind, steep time, plunge and decant timing.
  • Cold brew or espresso: use a method-specific recipe; do not force a filter-coffee ratio onto it.

Use grind to control flow and contact

Grind size changes surface area and, in many brewers, how quickly water moves through the bed. A finer grind can slow drawdown and increase extraction; a coarser grind can speed flow and reduce it. Burr alignment, particle spread, roast and the brewer also matter, so labels such as medium are not transferable between grinders. The repeatable unit is the named grinder and its recorded setting.

When the cup is sharply sour, thin or underdeveloped and the brew ran unusually fast, a modestly finer grind may be a useful next test. When it is drying, harsh or muddy and the brew stalled, a modestly coarser grind may help. These are diagnostic hypotheses, not flavor laws. Keep dose, water, temperature and pouring steady for the next brew so the result can actually test the grind change.

  • Change one or two marked grinder steps, not a large unrecorded turn.
  • Purge only as the grinder maker recommends; unnecessary purging wastes coffee.
  • Record total brew time as evidence, not as a target to hit at the expense of taste.
  • If results jump between identical settings, inspect technique, beans, burr condition and scale stability.

Control water and heat without false precision

Water is most of the beverage, so odor, taste and mineral content influence the cup and equipment. Use potable water that tastes clean, and follow the brewer maker's filtration and descaling instructions. Distilled or heavily softened water is not automatically ideal for every machine, and a filter pitcher does not guarantee a particular mineral composition. If water changes, record it as a recipe change.

Temperature guidance varies by method, roast and equipment. Many filter-coffee references use hot water below a rolling boil, while some automatic brewers control temperature internally. Measure only if the method and equipment make the reading meaningful. Do not wave a probe over unstable glassware or move a full kettle merely to create a number. A safe, consistent process is more useful than false one-degree precision.

  • Use the brewer's stated temperature or heating procedure as the first baseline.
  • Record water source and any filter or treatment, especially after a noticeable change.
  • Descale at the documented interval and method; do not improvise acids inside a warranted machine.
  • Keep kettles stable, cords away from edges and steam paths clear of hands and faces.

Taste with a decision tree

Let the cup cool enough to taste safely, then describe it in ordinary terms: sharp, sweet, hollow, dry, bitter, heavy, clean or muted. Separate strength—the concentration of the beverage—from extraction—the share of soluble material removed from the grounds. A cup can taste strong and sour or weak and dry. Ratio changes concentration directly; grind, temperature, agitation and contact time change extraction as well.

If the cup is balanced but too strong, keep the process and use slightly more water next time. If it is balanced but weak, use slightly less water. If it is sour and fast, test a finer grind; if it is dry and slow, test a coarser grind. If both flavor and flow are inconsistent, repeat the exact recipe before adjusting. The goal is not to obey a chart but to make the smallest change that answers one question.

  • Too strong but pleasant: increase water modestly while holding dose and grind constant.
  • Too weak but pleasant: decrease water modestly while holding dose and grind constant.
  • Fast and sharply sour: test a slightly finer grind with the same ratio and technique.
  • Slow and drying: test a slightly coarser grind after ruling out filter placement and channeling.

Keep a seven-line brew log

A useful log fits on one card: date and coffee; coffee dose; brew water and ratio; grinder and setting; water source and temperature; pour or steep sequence with total time; beverage yield and tasting decision. Add one line for the single next change. Photographs can supplement the record, but a picture of grounds cannot replace dose, water and time.

After three repeatable brews, save the recipe under the coffee and method rather than calling it the permanent setting. Beans age, humidity shifts and equipment changes. Clean the brewer and grinder according to their manuals, replace worn parts with compatible items and recalibrate or check the scale when readings drift. Good technique is a controlled loop: measure, brew, taste, change one thing and write down what happened.

  • Line 1: coffee, roast date if known and brew date.
  • Lines 2–5: dose, water, ratio, grinder setting, water and temperature.
  • Line 6: sequence, total time, beverage yield and any process fault.
  • Line 7: taste in plain language and exactly one change for the next brew.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good coffee-to-water ratio?

For many filter methods, 1:16 is a practical middle starting point: for example, 20 grams of coffee to 320 grams of brew water. Follow the exact brewer instructions and adjust to taste.

Should coffee ratio use water poured or coffee in the cup?

State which measurement you use. This guide uses brew water added for the ratio; beverage yield is recorded separately because grounds and equipment retain water.

What should change first when coffee tastes sour?

Check whether the brew also ran unusually fast and whether the recipe was followed. If so, test a modestly finer grind while keeping dose, water, temperature and technique steady.

Does stronger coffee always mean more extraction?

No. Strength is concentration, while extraction is how much soluble material left the grounds. Changing ratio and changing grind can move those dimensions differently.
