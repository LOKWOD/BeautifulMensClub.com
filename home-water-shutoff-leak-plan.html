The Home Water Shutoff Plan: Main Valve, Fixtures, Meter and Leak Response

The worst time to learn which valve controls the house is while water is crossing the floor. Build the map, label the boundaries and practice the decision before the leak.

Published September 20, 2026. Household planning information, not plumbing, electrical, insurance or legal advice. Follow utility, landlord, manufacturer and qualified-professional instructions for the property.

The useful answer

  • Identify the customer-side main shutoff, fixture valves, meter and any utility-owned control without assuming you are allowed to operate every valve you can see.
  • Build a one-page map with photographs, access notes, tool requirements and emergency contacts, then store copies where water cannot destroy them.
  • Use the water meter during a controlled no-use period to look for unexplained flow; a moving meter is a clue, not a diagnosis of location or cause.
  • During a leak, protect people first: avoid water near energized equipment, stop the source only when safe and within your authority, and call the right professional early.
  • Record the time, source, shutoff, damage, photographs, contacts and restoration so the household can improve the plan after the incident.

Draw the boundary before touching a valve

A home's water system can include a street or utility valve, meter, customer-side main shutoff, branch valves, fixture stops, appliance valves, irrigation controls, fire-suppression piping and, for a private well, a pump and pressure system. Ownership and operating authority vary. A valve being visible does not mean a resident should turn it.

Ask the water utility, landlord, building manager or qualified plumber to identify the utility boundary and the customer-controlled main. Record who confirmed it and when. In a condominium or multifamily building, one shutoff may affect other units or life-safety systems. Never experiment with a fire-sprinkler control, sealed utility valve or unidentified handle.

For a private well, include the electrical disconnect, pressure tank and service contact in the map, but do not improvise electrical work. The safe sequence depends on the equipment and failure. The plan should state who is authorized to operate each control and who to call when the leak is upstream of your valve or the valve does not move.

Build a room-by-room shutoff map

Walk from the service entry through the kitchen, bathrooms, laundry, water heater, exterior taps, irrigation, refrigerator, dishwasher, humidifier and any other plumbed appliance. Photograph each accessible shutoff in context and close up. Label the controlled fixture, normal position, access obstruction and the date a qualified person last confirmed operation.

Do not close old, corroded, leaking or unidentified valves merely to test them. A neglected valve can fail when moved. Ask a qualified plumber to inspect questionable controls and replace or exercise them under controlled conditions. The point of the audit is to reveal uncertainty before an emergency, not manufacture one on a quiet Sunday.

Create a one-page front sheet: main shutoff location, route from the entry door, flashlight location, whether a tool is authorized and required, utility emergency number, plumber, building management, insurer and restoration contact. Keep a waterproofed copy near—but not directly on—the main, another in the emergency document file and a secure digital copy.

Use the meter as a clue

EPA WaterSense recommends checking the water meter before and after a two-hour period when no water is being used; movement probably indicates a leak. Before the test, confirm that toilets, ice makers, irrigation, softeners, humidifiers and appliances will not intentionally draw water. Record the meter display or dial, exact times and any known automatic cycles.

A changed reading does not identify the leak or prove which party owns the problem. Repeat only under comparable controlled conditions and then involve the utility or a qualified plumber. Some meters transmit remotely, some show flow indicators and some are inaccessible to customers. Follow the utility's instructions rather than opening covers or entering unsafe spaces.

Track ordinary bills and meter readings for a useful baseline, but do not wait for a high bill when water is visible. Flow-monitoring devices and alerts can add information; they are not permission to leave a known leak unattended or a substitute for a working shutoff and human response plan.

Create a safe first-five-minutes response

First, protect people. If water is near an electrical panel, outlet, energized appliance, ceiling light or submerged cord, keep clear and call emergency services or the utility as conditions require. CPSC warns that mixing electricity and water can create shock, electrocution and fire hazards. Do not wade into water to unplug equipment or reach a valve.

If the scene is electrically safe and the source has a local fixture valve you are authorized to use, stop that source. Otherwise use the identified customer main if it can be reached and operated safely. Do not force a stuck handle, use an improvised extension or operate an unconfirmed utility control. Call the utility or plumber when the boundary, valve or cause is uncertain.

Move people and vulnerable property away from the active flow without entering a hazardous area. Use containers or towels only when that does not delay stopping the source or calling help. A ceiling that is bulging, contaminated water, structural damage, sewage, a hot-water release or a leak near electricity belongs with qualified responders, not a do-it-yourself experiment.

Document while the facts are fresh

Start an incident line with discovery time, observer, location, visible source, water appearance, odors, alarms and immediate hazards. Record which valve was operated, by whom and at what time; meter readings; utility or plumber calls; and when flow stopped. Take wide and close photographs from a safe position before moving contents when feasible.

List affected rooms, surfaces, furniture, electronics and documents without guessing at hidden damage or cause. Save invoices, work orders, damaged components retained with professional guidance and written recommendations. Contact the insurer promptly and follow its documentation and emergency-mitigation instructions; coverage and duties depend on the policy and facts.

EPA advises fixing plumbing leaks and drying items completely as soon as possible. Large water damage, sewage or contaminated water, significant mold, and health concerns call for professional help. Do not run a possibly contaminated HVAC system or close damp items into cabinets simply to make the room look recovered.

Restore service slowly and watch the system

Water should return only after the cause is controlled and the person responsible for the repair says restoration is appropriate. Confirm open faucets, disconnected appliances, damaged hoses and drain conditions as instructed. Reopen a valve slowly if the utility, plumber or manufacturer procedure calls for it; sudden pressure can reveal or worsen another weakness.

Walk the affected route while service returns. Watch connections, ceilings, walls, appliance pans and the meter. Listen for running toilets or hidden flow. If the meter continues to register unexplained use, shut down as directed and call the professional back. Do not conceal wet cavities or energize affected equipment before it has been evaluated.

Update the incident record with final readings, repairs, drying actions, photographs and the names of professionals. Schedule any follow-up inspection. A completed claim or dry-looking surface is not proof that plumbing, wiring, insulation and finishes are ready.

Run a twice-yearly desk drill

Twice a year, gather the household and walk—without unnecessarily operating valves—from the main entry to the customer shutoff. Point out fixture controls, the safe exit path, electrical no-go zones and the contact sheet. Make sure adults understand the difference between recognizing a valve and being authorized to turn it.

Check that labels remain readable, access is clear, contact numbers work, flashlights have power and photographs still match the property. Review seasonal exposures such as exterior hoses, vacant periods and freezing weather with the utility or plumber's current guidance. Do not use a generic internet temperature or dripping rule in place of local instructions and building-specific advice.

After renovations, appliance changes, a tenant turnover or any leak, revise the map. Add one sentence about what the event taught: the valve was hidden, the number was stale, the meter was hard to read or the plumber needed a model number. A useful plan is a maintained operational record, not a one-time checklist filed where no one can find it.

Use this printable shutoff record

For each control, write: system or fixture; exact location; photograph filename; normal position; who may operate it; whether a tool is permitted; identification confirmed by; confirmation date; condition; and service needed. For the main, add the utility boundary statement and a route description clear enough for a guest to follow under direction.

For the incident side, leave fields for discovery time, safety hazards, water source, first call, shutoff time, meter before and after, affected areas, photographs, professionals, insurer contact, drying actions and restoration approval. Keep the record factual. Unknown is better than inventing a cause for a contractor or claim handler.

Finally, connect the sheet to the home's larger safety system: emergency documents, power-outage plan, fire escape plan, home inventory and guest briefing. Water damage becomes more manageable when the shutoff decision, records and contacts already have a place.

Frequently asked questions

Where is the main water shutoff?

It varies by building and climate. Ask the utility, landlord, building manager or qualified plumber to identify the customer-controlled main and the utility boundary; do not guess from appearance.

Should I test an old shutoff valve?

Do not force or casually operate an old, corroded, leaking or unidentified valve. Have a qualified plumber inspect and exercise or replace it under controlled conditions.

Can a smart leak detector replace the shutoff plan?

No. Sensors and automatic controls can add alerts or action, but they need exact installation, maintenance and a human response path. They do not resolve ownership, electrical hazards or a failed valve.

What if water is near electricity?

Stay clear. Do not enter water or reach for plugs, panels or valves in the hazard area. Call emergency services, the electric utility or another qualified responder as conditions require.
