Medical Bill and EOB Review File: Match, Appeal and Close Out

An explanation of benefits is not a bill, a provider statement is not proof that the plan processed the claim correctly, and a phone promise is not a closed account. Put the EOB, itemized bill, payment history and denial notice on one ledger, ask one precise question at a time, and keep the file open until the written records agree.

Published November 10, 2026. General U.S. consumer organization, not medical, legal, insurance, coding, tax or financial advice. Coverage, appeal rights, deadlines and surprise-billing protections depend on the plan, service, state and facts. Use the current EOB, denial notice, plan document and official assistance route for the specific claim.

The useful answer

  • Treat the EOB as the plan's claim explanation and the provider bill as a separate request for payment; CMS says an EOB is not a bill.
  • Match patient, provider, service date, claim number, line item, billed charge, allowed amount, plan payment, adjustments and patient responsibility before paying a disputed balance.
  • Ask the provider for an itemized bill and the plan for the claim detail or denial reason; do not guess what an adjustment or code means.
  • Follow the deadline and submission route printed on the actual denial notice or plan document; federal examples are not a substitute for plan-specific instructions.
  • Close the file only after the provider ledger, plan record, payments, credits, appeal decision and remaining balance reconcile to the cent.

Open one claim file before making another payment

Give the case a neutral ID built from the patient initials, provider, service date and claim number. Save the EOB, every provider statement, receipts, portal messages, denial letters and notes in date order. Record whether the coverage was Marketplace, individual, employer fully insured, employer self-funded, Medicare, Medicaid or something else, because the regulator and review route can differ. Do not place diagnoses, member IDs or full account numbers in an unencrypted shared note.

Start the file even when the amount looks small. A copay collected at the visit, a deposit, a later card payment and an insurer payment can appear on different systems. Write each movement in a payment ledger with date, payer, payee, amount, method, confirmation and the claim or invoice it was supposed to cover. The purpose is not to challenge every charge; it is to stop two valid-looking records from drifting apart.

  • Case ID, plan type and member-service route: ________
  • Provider account and claim number, partially masked: ________
  • Service date, facility and named professionals: ________
  • Documents saved and sensitive-storage location: ________

Read the EOB as a claim map, not an invoice

CMS describes the EOB as a notice showing the charges, what the plan covers and what the patient may owe; it is not itself a bill. Copy the patient, provider, service date, claim number, each service line, billed charge, allowed amount, plan payment, adjustment and patient-responsibility category. If the EOB uses a remark or reason code, use the plan's legend or ask member services for the plain-language meaning. Do not translate a code from an online forum.

Separate deductible, copayment, coinsurance, noncovered amount and any amount attributed to network status. A zero plan payment does not automatically mean the patient owes the full charge: the claim may be denied, pending, duplicated, bundled, sent to another payer or reduced by the contracted rate. Likewise, an allowed amount is not necessarily the amount already paid. Preserve the EOB version because a reprocessed claim may generate a second notice.

  • EOB issue date and claim status: ________
  • Billed / allowed / plan paid / patient responsibility: ________
  • Adjustment and reason codes with official meaning: ________
  • Pending, denied, reprocessed or final: ________

Match the itemized provider bill line by line

Ask for an itemized bill when the statement shows only a total. Match the patient, service date, place of service, provider, description, quantity and amount against the EOB. A single visit can produce separate professional, facility, laboratory, imaging, anesthesia or pathology claims, so do not assume every separate envelope is a duplicate. Instead, give each bill its own row and link it to the corresponding claim number.

CMS advises comparing the medical bill with the EOB and checking that the billed amount agrees with the EOB's patient responsibility. Add every payment and credit already posted by the provider. If a bill arrived before an EOB, ask the plan whether it received the claim, except for cost sharing collected at the visit. Mark a difference as unresolved until the provider or plan explains it in writing or corrects the record.

  • Bill date, invoice number and itemized version received: ________
  • Matching EOB claim number and service lines: ________
  • Provider payments and credits posted: ________
  • Expected balance / billed balance / difference: ________

Classify the mismatch before choosing the contact

Use a small set of categories: identity or date error, missing or duplicate claim, missing payment, wrong plan, network-status question, coding or quantity question, noncovered benefit, medical-necessity denial, prior-authorization issue, coordination-of-benefits problem, surprise-billing concern or unexplained provider balance. This is a routing label, not a diagnosis or accusation. Ask the provider about its bill and ledger; ask the plan about claim processing, coverage and appeal rights.

Do not ask billing staff to make a clinical judgment or ask the insurer to rewrite the provider's record. If a code or quantity appears wrong, request that the provider review the source documentation and resubmit when appropriate. If the EOB says the provider is out of network but the directory or pre-service confirmation showed otherwise, preserve screenshots, dates and names. If the problem may fall under the No Surprises Act, use CMS's current action plan rather than assuming every unexpected charge qualifies.

  • Mismatch category and exact line: ________
  • Who owns the next answer: provider / plan / employer / regulator
  • Question stated without accusation: ________
  • Correction, reprocessing, explanation or appeal requested: ________

Run calls and portal messages as an evidence log

Before contacting anyone, write the claim number, one discrepancy and the result you need. Record the date, time, channel, representative name or ID, department, reference number, what each person said and the promised next step. Repeat back the action and deadline. After a call, send a secure portal message summarizing the agreement when available. Never send protected health information through an ordinary public social-media message.

If the provider agrees to place the account on hold while a claim is reviewed, record the hold end date and ask whether statements, late fees or collection activity will pause. A hold is not forgiveness and a verbal adjustment is not a posted credit. If the plan says the provider must correct and resubmit, ask for the exact rejection or denial reason and the claim record that supports it. Escalate politely when the first contact cannot see the required system.

  • Contact, department, date and reference: ________
  • Question asked and answer received: ________
  • Promised action, owner and due date: ________
  • Written confirmation or follow-up date: ________

Build an appeal packet around the actual denial notice

CMS says people have a right to appeal when a private health plan refuses to pay a bill, and the route can differ for self-funded and fully insured employer plans. Read the denial notice and the plan's claims procedure. Write down the filing deadline, address or portal, permitted representative, internal-review level and any urgent-review route. HealthCare.gov gives 180 days and other timelines for the federal framework it describes, but the controlling instruction for a particular case is the current notice and plan document.

Index the EOB, denial notice, itemized bill, plan provision, relevant provider documentation, prior-authorization record, referral, directory evidence and correspondence. Keep originals and submit copies unless the instructions require a signed original form. The appeal should identify the claim, quote the denial reason accurately, state the requested result and connect each exhibit to that request. If the issue depends on medical judgment, work with the treating professional rather than inventing a clinical argument.

  • Denial reason quoted exactly: ________
  • Internal appeal deadline, route and proof: ________
  • Exhibits and what each proves: ________
  • External review or assistance route shown on notice: ________

Close the account only after all ledgers agree

When a claim is reprocessed or an appeal is decided, obtain the new EOB and the provider's updated ledger. Compare them with the decision letter and prior payments. Confirm that reversals, contractual adjustments, refunds and credits posted to the right service date. If money is still owed, ask for a final itemized statement and payment receipt. If the patient paid too much, record who will refund it, the amount, method and expected date.

Close each discrepancy with a written outcome: corrected, explained and accepted, appealed and paid, externally reviewed, financial assistance granted, payment plan established, referred for qualified help or unresolved. Keep the retention period required by the plan, tax records, benefit account or legal advice rather than following a universal number. Set a follow-up date if any balance, refund or corrected EOB remains pending.

  • Final EOB and provider ledger compared: ________
  • Total patient payments, refunds and final balance: ________
  • Appeal or review result and date: ________
  • Closure status, retention reason and follow-up: ________

Frequently asked questions

Is an EOB the same as a medical bill?

No. CMS says an EOB is a health-plan notice that explains charges, coverage and possible patient responsibility; it is not a bill. Pay only a provider or facility bill after matching it to the plan record and posted payments.

Should I pay a bill that arrived before the EOB?

CMS advises getting the EOB and comparing it with the bill, except that a provider may collect a copayment or coinsurance at the time of service. Ask the plan whether it received the claim and ask the provider about any hold while the record is reviewed.

How long do I have to appeal?

Use the deadline in the denial notice and plan document. HealthCare.gov describes a 180-day internal-appeal deadline in its federal framework, but plan type, governing law and the specific review can change the controlling route or timing.

Who can help if the plan still denies the claim?

The final denial should describe external review when available. A state Consumer Assistance Program or insurance department, an employer benefits office for a job-based plan, CMS help routes or a qualified attorney or patient advocate may be appropriate depending on the plan and issue.
