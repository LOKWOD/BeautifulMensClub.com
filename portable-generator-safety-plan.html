Portable Generator Safety Plan: Placement, Fuel, Cords and Carbon Monoxide

A portable generator is an outdoor engine with exhaust, fuel and electrical hazards—not an indoor appliance. Map the site, loads and shutdown rules before an outage removes your margin for improvisation.

Published September 30, 2026. General preparedness information, not electrical, fire, medical or code advice. Follow the exact manual, local authorities, utilities and qualified professionals. For suspected carbon-monoxide poisoning, move to fresh air and call 911.

The useful answer

  • Operate a portable generator outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the home, with exhaust directed away from windows, doors and vents.
  • Never plug a generator into a wall outlet. Permanent home connection requires approved transfer equipment installed by a qualified electrician.
  • Use outdoor-rated cords and a written load budget; wattage and connector compatibility must match the exact generator and equipment.
  • Install working carbon-monoxide alarms, cool the generator before refueling and record model, serial, recall and maintenance information.

Build the emergency gate first

Carbon monoxide is colorless and odorless. Headache, dizziness, weakness, nausea, vomiting, chest pain and confusion can be signs of poisoning. If an alarm sounds or symptoms appear, move everyone to fresh air immediately, call 911 and do not reenter to silence equipment or retrieve belongings.

Do not wait for a person to collapse before treating the event as urgent. Emergency responders and clinicians decide when the space is safe and what care is needed. A generator’s carbon-monoxide shutoff feature is a backup, not permission to move the machine closer or indoors.

  • Post the emergency address and 911 instruction where every operator can see it.
  • Name a meeting place in fresh air away from the exhaust route.
  • Record who will account for children, guests, older adults and pets.
  • Never send someone back inside because symptoms seem to improve outdoors.

Map a legal outdoor placement before the storm

CPSC and CDC guidance says portable generators belong outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the house, with exhaust pointed away from the home and other buildings. Never use one in a house, garage, basement, shed, crawlspace, porch, carport or other enclosed or partly enclosed area—even with doors open.

Draw the exact spot and measure it. Mark nearby windows, doors, vents, neighboring openings, combustible materials, water flow and the cord route. Follow the manufacturer’s weather instructions; a canopy or cover that traps exhaust can turn an outdoor machine into a carbon-monoxide hazard.

  • Measure 20 feet or more from the home; do not estimate by pacing during an outage.
  • Point exhaust away from every occupied structure and opening.
  • Keep the unit on a stable surface where rainwater cannot pool and people cannot trip over cords.
  • Check local fire, noise and property rules without compromising the federal safety guidance.

Choose the connection system before choosing loads

Never plug a generator into a wall outlet. Backfeeding can energize utility lines and injure or kill utility workers, neighbors and household members. A generator connected to household wiring needs listed, compatible transfer equipment designed and installed by a qualified electrician under applicable code and utility rules.

For cord-connected use, start from the manual’s receptacle ratings and grounding instructions. Use intact outdoor-rated cords whose conductor size, current rating, plug and length suit the load. Keep every connection dry in the manner the manual permits, and do not modify plugs or defeat ground pins.

  • Label each cord with its rating, length and intended appliance.
  • Replace cords with cuts, crushed jackets, hot connectors, bent blades or taped repairs.
  • Use GFCI protection exactly as the generator manual and applicable code require.
  • Have an electrician document the transfer device, inlet, neutral and grounding arrangement.

Write a load budget in running and starting watts

List only critical equipment: refrigeration, a few lights, communications, medical equipment where professionally planned, pumps or limited heating controls. Record running watts and any higher starting or surge requirement from the equipment label or manufacturer. Leave operating margin instead of treating the generator’s headline wattage as a target.

Some loads require special power quality, connection or startup planning. Do not assume that every furnace, well pump, electronic control or medical device can run safely from a portable generator. Ask the equipment maker and a qualified professional before the outage.

  • Start the generator with loads disconnected unless the manual says otherwise.
  • Add critical loads one at a time while watching for overload indications and abnormal sound.
  • Do not use a power strip to exceed a receptacle, cord or generator rating.
  • Record the shutdown priority so no one guesses when fuel runs low.

Handle fuel as a separate fire plan

Store the exact permitted fuel in approved, clearly labeled containers outside living areas and away from ignition sources, following local fire rules and the generator manual. Record purchase and rotation dates. Never store fuel in a basement, living space or beside the running machine.

Turn the generator off and let it cool before refueling. Fuel spilled on a hot engine can ignite. Use the manufacturer’s filling procedure, avoid overfilling and clean spills only when the area is safe. Never smoke or create sparks around fuel.

  • Match fuel type and any stabilizer to the current manual; do not improvise blends.
  • Transport and store containers upright and secured from children.
  • Keep a compatible fire extinguisher accessible only if household members know its limits and use.
  • Arrange lawful disposal for old fuel; never pour it onto soil, drains or household trash.

Use a start, run and shutdown record

Before each run, check the model and serial against the CPSC recall database, inspect the machine and cords, test carbon-monoxide alarms and clear the placement. Start and operate exactly as the manual instructs. Keep children and untrained operators away, and never leave a running generator where changes cannot be noticed.

Log start time, fuel added, loads connected, maintenance hours, alarms and any fault. To shut down, remove loads in the manual’s sequence, stop the machine and let it cool. After the outage, service and store it according to the manual so the next operator inherits a known condition.

  • Record brand, model, serial number, manual location and recall-check date.
  • Photograph the measured placement and cord route in daylight.
  • Test alarms on the manufacturer’s schedule and replace units at end of life.
  • Stop for exhaust movement toward a building, a carbon-monoxide alarm, fuel leak, damaged cord, overload or abnormal operation.

Frequently asked questions

Can a portable generator run in a garage with the door open?

No. CPSC and CDC say portable generators must be operated outdoors only, at least 20 feet from the home, with exhaust directed away from windows, doors and vents.

Can I connect a generator through a wall outlet?

Never. That can backfeed household and utility lines. A home connection requires approved transfer equipment installed by a qualified electrician under applicable rules.

Is a built-in carbon-monoxide shutoff enough?

No. Treat it as a backup. Correct outdoor placement, exhaust direction and working household carbon-monoxide alarms remain essential.

Should I refuel while the generator is running?

No. Shut it down and allow it to cool according to the manual before refueling. Fuel spilled on a hot engine can ignite.
