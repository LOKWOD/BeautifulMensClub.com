Doctor Visit Preparation and Follow-Up Checklist: Medicines, Questions and Next Steps

A good appointment file does not diagnose the problem or script the answer. It gives the care team an exact medicine list, a usable timeline, the records that matter and one clear question—then turns the visit into a plan you can actually follow.

Published October 27, 2026. General appointment-organization guidance, not medical advice, diagnosis, treatment or an emergency service. Do not start, stop or change a medicine or delay urgent care because of this checklist. Use 911 or the appropriate emergency service for an emergency.

The useful answer

  • Write the decision you need from the visit before collecting background information.
  • Bring every prescription, nonprescription medicine, vitamin, supplement and relevant topical, inhaled or injected product.
  • Turn symptoms into a dated timeline with function, measurements and context instead of guessing at a diagnosis.
  • Ask who owns each next step, what warning sign changes the plan and when follow-up will occur.
  • Leave with a written record you can repeat back accurately, then update it when results or instructions arrive.

Start with the one decision this visit must support

Write one sentence at the top of the page: “At this visit I need help deciding ______.” A checkup, new symptom, medicine review, test result, procedure discussion and follow-up all require different preparation. A short decision statement protects the visit from becoming a tour of every concern you have ever had. Add a ranked list of two secondary questions, then keep a parking-lot list for issues that deserve another appointment rather than squeezing them into the final minute.

Record who requested the appointment, the clinician and specialty, location, date, arrival instructions and whether the visit is in person or remote. Confirm referral, authorization, copay, interpretation, accessibility and records-transfer needs before the day. Those administrative checks are not medical conclusions, but a missing referral or inaccessible portal can consume the time meant for care. If a symptom is urgent or rapidly worsening, use the care team's urgent route instead of waiting for a routine slot.

  • Primary decision or question: ________
  • Two secondary questions: ________
  • Visit type, clinician, date and access details: ________
  • Urgent-care or emergency boundary confirmed: ________

Build the complete medicine and product list

AHRQ advises bringing all medicines, including prescriptions, nonprescription products, vitamins and dietary or herbal supplements. Include creams, ointments, patches, drops, sprays, inhalers and injections—not only pills. For each item, record the exact name, strength, form, amount, schedule, reason used, prescriber and what you actually take. Add recent starts, stops, missed doses or changes. Bring original containers when the care team requests them, or clear photographs of every label and Drug Facts panel.

Keep allergies and prior reactions separate from ordinary side effects, and describe what happened rather than writing only a category name. List alcohol, nicotine and other substances when they are relevant to the question. Do not simplify the page by hiding a product or stopping a prescribed medicine before the visit. The value of the record is completeness. Date the list and preserve the old version so a later reviewer can see what changed rather than treating the newest list as timeless.

  • Prescription medicines and actual use: ________
  • OTC medicines, vitamins, herbs and supplements: ________
  • Every route, allergy and prior reaction: ________
  • Recent change, missed dose or uncertain instruction: ________

Turn the problem into a timeline, not a theory

Record when the change began, what was happening before it, how often it occurs, how long it lasts and whether it is stable, improving or worsening. Use plain observations: location, sensation, appearance, severity in your own consistent scale and what ordinary activity changed. Note sleep, food, activity, illness, travel, injury and new products only as context. “Headache began at 3 p.m. after the new schedule” is a record; “the schedule caused it” is a conclusion for the clinician to evaluate.

Bring measurements only when the device, method and timestamps are known. A home reading can help show a pattern but does not diagnose or clear a condition. Photograph visible changes consistently, preserve relevant test reports and list the questions each document raises. If there are warning symptoms, use the urgent instructions already provided by your care team or emergency services. The checklist must never become a reason to keep documenting while a person needs immediate help.

  • First noticed and change over time: ________
  • Frequency, duration and functional effect: ________
  • Measurements, photos or reports with dates: ________
  • Red flag and escalation route: ________

Bring the smallest useful evidence packet

Create a one-page summary and attach only records that support the visit question: medicine list, relevant recent results, a short symptom timeline, prior instruction, procedure report or specialist note. Do not assume every clinician can see every outside system. Confirm whether records arrived and whether images, laboratory reports or visit notes are needed. Keep personal identifiers protected and use the office's approved transfer route rather than ordinary email when sensitive information is involved.

Bring an updated insurance card, identification and contact details when the office requires them, but do not turn the packet into a portable identity vault. A trusted support person can help listen, take notes or describe observed change when the patient wants that help. Agree in advance on the person's role and privacy boundaries. For a remote visit, test camera, microphone, connection and the way you will show labels or movement without improvising after the appointment begins.

  • One-page summary completed: ________
  • Outside records confirmed received: ________
  • Support person and role: ________
  • Secure transfer or telehealth test: ________

Ask questions that end in an owner and a date

Use the visit to clarify evidence, alternatives and action. Ask what the leading possibilities are, what information would change the assessment, what the recommended next step is and what reasonable alternatives exist. For a medicine, ask its purpose, how and when to take it, important interactions or side effects, what to do about a missed dose and how effectiveness will be judged. For a test or referral, ask what question it is meant to answer and who will communicate the result.

Before leaving, use teach-back: explain the plan in your own words and ask the clinician to correct it. Record every action beside an owner and due date—patient, office, pharmacy, laboratory, insurer or specialist. Ask what symptom or result should trigger an earlier call, urgent care or emergency help. If instructions conflict with an older plan or another clinician's advice, ask for reconciliation rather than silently choosing the answer that is easiest to follow.

  • What the team thinks and what remains uncertain: ________
  • Action, owner and due date: ________
  • Monitoring and warning signs: ________
  • Teach-back corrected and recorded: ________

Close the loop after the appointment

On the same day, update the medicine list, calendar every follow-up and store the visit summary with the source records. Verify prescriptions, referrals and test orders rather than assuming they transmitted successfully. Record the office's route for questions and the expected result timeline. If a portal summary differs from what you understood, ask promptly and preserve the clarification. Do not edit the record to make the visit look cleaner; a dated correction is more useful than a rewritten history.

Track whether the planned action occurred and what changed afterward without declaring success or failure from one day. If a result does not arrive by the stated date, follow the office's process and log the contact. Bring the updated file to the next visit. The authority asset is the continuity: one question, complete inputs, explicit decisions, owners, dates and a documented close. It supports care without pretending that paperwork can replace professional judgment.

  • Medicines and instructions updated: ________
  • Orders, referrals and results route verified: ________
  • Follow-up date and earlier-call trigger: ________
  • Open item closed or escalated on: ________

Frequently asked questions

Should I bring every medicine bottle?

Follow the office's instructions. AHRQ materials recommend bringing all medicines, including nonprescription products, vitamins, supplements and products used by other routes.

How many questions should I ask?

Prioritize one decision and two secondary questions. Put lower-priority issues on a separate list so the clinician can help decide what needs another visit.

Can a family member come with me?

Often yes, subject to office rules and your consent. Agree on whether that person will listen, take notes, help with history or ask questions.

What if symptoms become severe before the appointment?

Use the clinician's urgent route or emergency services as appropriate. Do not wait for a scheduled visit or keep completing a worksheet during an emergency.
