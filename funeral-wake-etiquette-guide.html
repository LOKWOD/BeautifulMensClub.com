Funeral and Wake Etiquette: What to Wear, Say, Bring and Do

You do not need the perfect sentence or a performance of grief. You need accurate information, quiet clothes, a short sincere acknowledgment and the discipline to make the day easier for the family.

Published September 19, 2026. Etiquette guidance, not a substitute for the family's stated wishes, faith tradition, venue rules, grief counseling or legal and funeral-service advice.

The useful answer

  • Read the obituary or family notice before asking questions it already answers; service type, dress, flowers, donations and privacy wishes may be stated there.
  • Wear subdued, clean clothing appropriate to the venue and tradition; black is safe, but respect matters more than theatrical mourning clothes.
  • Say the person's name, acknowledge the loss and keep the first exchange brief enough that the family can receive others.
  • Do not photograph, post, livestream, tag locations or share private details unless the family has clearly invited it.
  • Useful follow-up after the crowd thins often matters more than finding a grand gesture on the day.

Get the facts before entering the room

Funeral, memorial service, celebration of life, wake, visitation, calling hours, burial and shiva are not interchangeable events. Read the full obituary, funeral-home notice or direct family message. Confirm date, time, address, whether the event is public or private, parking, dress guidance, flowers or donation preference, reception details and any religious or cultural instructions.

Use the family's stated language. If the notice calls it a celebration of life, do not correct it into a funeral. If it requests family-only burial or no flowers, treat that as a boundary rather than a suggestion. When something important remains unclear, ask the venue or a designated contact instead of making the closest grieving person manage logistics.

Plan the route, parking and childcare early. Arriving rushed leads to whispering, seat changes and attention that belongs elsewhere. Bring only what you can manage quietly: phone on silent, tissues, the card or item the notice invited, and clothing appropriate to weather and walking conditions.

Dress for respect, not costume

Black, charcoal, navy and other subdued colors are dependable when no guidance is given. A dark suit with a quiet shirt and tie is appropriate for many traditional services, but clean dark trousers with a jacket or sweater can be respectful where formality is lower. The family, faith tradition, region and venue set the real standard.

Avoid loud patterns, slogans, distressed clothing, clubwear, novelty ties, excessive fragrance and anything that requires constant adjustment. Choose shoes you can stand and walk in safely, including on grass, gravel, snow or cemetery ground. Outerwear should be presentable because it may remain on for graveside portions.

Some families request color, team clothing, uniforms or something connected to the deceased. Follow that invitation without turning it into a costume. If a house of worship has expectations for head coverings, shoulders, footwear or seating, comply quietly or ask the venue before arriving.

Use a short, honest first sentence

The job is acknowledgment, not explanation. 'I am so sorry. I cared about David, and I am thinking of you' is enough. Use the deceased person's name when it feels natural. A short specific memory can be valuable, especially when it reveals kindness, humor or impact the family may not have heard.

Avoid trying to solve the loss with certainty: do not declare that everything happens for a reason, that the person is in a better place, or that you know exactly how someone feels. Those phrases can conflict with belief, circumstances and grief. Do not ask for medical details, speculate about cause, compare tragedies or turn the exchange into your own story.

If words fail, say so plainly: 'I don't have the right words, but I am very sorry and I am here.' Let silence exist. A handshake, brief hug or touch should follow the other person's cue; grief does not erase consent or personal space.

Move through the visitation without creating work

At a wake or visitation, sign the register clearly enough to be read and include a relationship or organization when that helps the family identify you later. Join the receiving line, keep the first conversation concise and step aside when others are waiting. You can reconnect more fully at a reception or in the weeks afterward.

An open casket or viewing is optional unless your role or tradition clearly makes it otherwise. You may approach briefly, pause from a respectful distance or bypass it without commentary. Do not touch the body, rearrange objects or place anything in the casket without explicit direction from the family or funeral staff.

Observe before acting. Follow ushers, clergy and funeral-home staff on seating, kneeling, standing, procession and dismissal. If you do not share the religious practice, remain quietly respectful; no one needs a visible debate about belief in that room.

Make the service easier to conduct

Arrive before the stated start time, silence the phone completely and put away earbuds and smart-watch alerts. Sit where ushers direct. Keep aisles clear, supervise children and avoid leaving during readings or remarks unless someone genuinely needs assistance.

Do not photograph the casket, family, mourners, clergy, register, flowers or private messages without clear permission. Never livestream or post a location because it seems public. An obituary is not blanket consent to document the people attending or repeat information shared in the room.

If invited to speak, confirm the time limit and audience. Prepare one clear memory, write it down and stop when the point is made. Avoid private jokes that need explanation, disputed family history, grievances, intoxication stories, business promotion and any disclosure the deceased or family would reasonably have kept private.

Handle flowers, donations, food and cards precisely

Follow the notice. 'In lieu of flowers' usually directs attention to a charity or cause; use the exact organization and memorial information supplied. Do not substitute a personal campaign or assume the family wants physical gifts delivered to the service. Keep receipts and confirmation details private unless a card is specifically requested.

A sympathy card should identify you, acknowledge the loss and offer a true sentence about the person or family. If you offer help, make it concrete and low-pressure: a meal on a named evening, school pickup, lawn care, pet care or handling a specific errand. 'Anything you need' sounds generous but transfers the planning back to the grieving person.

Coordinate food through the named organizer or meal train. Ask about allergies, refrigeration, containers and delivery timing. Use a disposable or clearly labeled container, include heating instructions and do not expect a visit when dropping it off. The useful gift is nourishment without another obligation.

Bring children with a plan, not a surprise

Consider the child's age, relationship, wishes, the family's guidance and the event. Explain in direct, age-appropriate language what the room, casket, rituals and emotions may look like. Avoid euphemisms that confuse death with ordinary sleep or travel. Give the child permission to ask questions and to step out with a designated adult.

Sit near an exit, bring one silent comfort item if appropriate and decide who will leave with the child without disrupting the service. Do not shame a child for crying, fidgeting or not appearing sad enough. Also do not make the bereaved family reassure the child while they are receiving guests.

If the child does not attend, another form of participation may fit: drawing a card, choosing a photo, sharing a memory or visiting the family later. The goal is honest inclusion and support, not forcing a performance for adults.

Know where consumer questions begin

If you are helping arrange or pay for a funeral, etiquette is no longer the only issue. The Federal Trade Commission's Funeral Rule gives consumers specific rights when buying funeral goods and services, including itemized price information and choices about goods and services. Read the current FTC guidance and applicable state rules rather than relying on family assumptions or verbal bundles.

Keep emotional support separate from purchasing pressure. A friend can take notes, organize questions and help compare the written General Price List without dictating what grief should cost. Do not present your taste as a moral requirement or imply that a less expensive choice means less love.

Veteran benefits, cemetery rules, religious requirements, transportation, death certificates and insurance each have their own authority. Refer those questions to the relevant funeral director, cemetery, clergy, government program, attorney or insurer instead of improvising.

Follow up after the room empties

Support often drops sharply after the service. Put a reminder on the calendar for one or two weeks later, then again around a meaningful date. Send a simple message that does not demand a reply: 'Thinking of you today. No need to respond.' Mentioning the person's name can be a kindness rather than a forbidden subject.

Repeat practical offers when you can actually deliver them. Grief has no universal schedule, and the VA National Center for PTSD notes that there is no single right way or normal length of time to grieve. Avoid diagnosing someone's grief or setting a deadline for recovery. Encourage professional help when the person asks for it or when safety is a concern.

Keep the relationship honest. Invite the person to ordinary life without punishing a decline, remember anniversaries if you were close enough to know them, and share memories privately. The standard is not a perfect funeral-day performance. It is steady respect before, during and after the public moment.

Frequently asked questions

Do men have to wear a black suit to a funeral?

No. A dark, clean, subdued outfit appropriate to the venue and family's guidance is usually respectful. Black is a safe option, not a universal law.

What should I say to the family?

Acknowledge the loss, use the person's name and keep it sincere: 'I am very sorry. I cared about Maria and I am thinking of you.' A short memory is welcome when appropriate.

Is it okay not to approach an open casket?

Yes. You can pause at a respectful distance or bypass the viewing quietly. Follow the family's and venue's customs without announcing your choice.

Should I send flowers or make a donation?

Follow the obituary or family notice. Use the exact charity or flower instructions provided, and do not substitute a different campaign without asking.
