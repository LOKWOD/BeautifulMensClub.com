Home Emergency Kit Rotation Checklist: Water, Food, Power and Documents

An emergency kit is only useful if the water is there, the batteries work, the food is edible and everyone knows where the important pieces are.

Published September 18, 2026. General U.S. preparedness guidance. Follow local emergency management, utility, medical and public-safety instructions for actual emergencies.

The useful answer

  • Start with several days of water and shelf-stable food, then add light, communication, first aid, sanitation, basic tools and local information.
  • Ready.gov recommends one gallon of water per person per day for at least three days as a basic supply-kit benchmark, then adjusting for household needs.
  • Customize the kit for medications, children, older adults, disabilities, pets and any equipment your household depends on.
  • Store supplies where household members can reach them, and split critical items when one location could become inaccessible.
  • Put recurring checks on the calendar so batteries, medications, documents, food and seasonal gear are replaced before they fail.

Build for the problems you can actually face

A home emergency kit should make a short disruption easier to manage without turning the house into a survival warehouse. Start with events that can realistically interrupt normal services where you live: power loss, severe weather, temporary water problems, evacuation orders, road closures or a few days when stores and pharmacies are difficult to reach. The kit should cover essential needs while official services recover.

Ready.gov's basic supply guidance centers on water, nonperishable food, a battery-powered or hand-crank radio, flashlight, first-aid supplies, extra batteries, whistle, sanitation items, charging capability, local maps and basic tools. Those categories are more important than buying one branded emergency kit whose contents you have never inspected.

Separate shelter-at-home supplies from items you would need to leave quickly. A larger home reserve can be heavier and more comfortable, while a small grab-ready bag should contain documents, medications, communication, light and a practical amount of food and water. Do not make one container so heavy that the intended person cannot move it.

Set the water and food baseline

Ready.gov materials use one gallon of water per person per day for at least three days as a basic benchmark for drinking and sanitation. More may be needed for hot weather, pregnancy, illness, children, pets or medical needs. Store water according to container instructions, protect it from chemicals and heat, and rotate supplies by marked dates and condition.

Choose food that your household will actually eat, can open and can prepare with little or no cooking. Shelf-stable meals, canned foods, nut or seed butters, crackers, bars and other familiar items can work. Include a manual can opener when canned food is part of the plan. Track allergies and special dietary requirements rather than assuming any emergency-food box fits everyone.

Rotation is easier when emergency food overlaps with the normal pantry. Put the oldest compatible items into regular meals and replace them with fresh stock. That reduces waste and makes it obvious whether anyone will actually eat the supplies. Keep a written inventory with quantities and dates so the kit does not become a mystery tote.

Keep power, light and information redundant

A flashlight should be immediately reachable without walking through a dark house to find the emergency kit. Keep additional lighting in the main kit along with spare batteries that match the devices. Battery lanterns can make a room functional, while open flames add fire risk and are a poor default around children, pets, gas leaks or unstable conditions.

Maintain at least two ways to receive information. A charged phone is useful, but networks can be congested and batteries run down. Ready.gov includes a battery-powered or hand-crank radio capable of receiving NOAA Weather Radio alerts in its basic-kit guidance. Know which local stations and official alert systems serve your area, and keep a paper list of important numbers.

Power banks should be charged on a schedule, not discovered empty during an outage. Label them with the month they were checked. If your household depends on powered medical equipment, refrigeration for medication, a well pump or other essential systems, the emergency plan needs a specific backup strategy developed with the relevant manufacturer, utility or medical professional rather than assuming a consumer battery pack will cover it.

Add health, sanitation and household-specific needs

Ready.gov emphasizes customizing supplies for the people in the household. Include necessary prescription information, basic first-aid supplies, hygiene items, eyeglasses or contact-lens needs, and any approved over-the-counter items your household normally uses. Medication storage and emergency refill rules vary, so ask the prescriber or pharmacist how to prepare safely rather than stockpiling improperly.

Children may need formula, diapers, comfort items or age-appropriate food. Older adults or people with disabilities may need mobility supplies, hearing-aid batteries, communication cards or backup equipment. Pets need food, water, medications, carriers or leashes, waste supplies and veterinary information. Write these needs into the inventory instead of trusting memory.

Sanitation matters when water or sewer service is disrupted. Store soap or sanitizer, toilet paper, trash bags, wipes and household-specific hygiene products. A simple cleanup plan can prevent a manageable outage from becoming a health problem. Keep chemicals, fuel and medicines secured and separated from food and children's access.

Protect the documents that make recovery easier

Keep copies or secure digital access to identification, insurance information, emergency contacts, medication lists, key account contacts and important household records. The goal is not to carry every original paper; it is to have enough verified information to identify people, contact insurers and providers, and make informed decisions when normal systems are unavailable.

Use a water-resistant pouch for physical copies and a secure, encrypted method for digital backups. Review access at least annually so old phone numbers, expired policies or forgotten passwords do not become the plan. Do not store the only copy of a critical document in the same place as the original.

Include some cash in small denominations if that fits your household because electronic payment systems can be unavailable during outages. Also keep a local paper map and a written meeting and contact plan. Those items seem old-fashioned until devices are dead or connectivity is poor.

Put rotation on the calendar

Assign a fixed review cadence instead of relying on memory. A quick quarterly check can confirm power banks, flashlight batteries, radio function, visible damage and seasonal gear. A deeper twice-yearly review can rotate food and water, verify medication and first-aid dates, check clothing sizes for children, update documents and confirm that everyone knows the kit locations.

Use the same months every year, such as the start of spring and fall. Write the next review date on the container. During each check, test rather than merely look: turn on the radio, charge the battery pack, open the flashlight compartment for corrosion, inspect seals and read dates. Replace anything questionable immediately.

Finally, rehearse one realistic scenario. Turn off the lights for an hour, locate the kit, power a phone, find the paper contacts and make a simple meal without normal appliances. A short drill reveals missing adapters, dead batteries, inaccessible storage and unrealistic assumptions far faster than another shopping list.

Frequently asked questions

How much water should a home emergency kit include?

Ready.gov uses one gallon per person per day for at least three days as a basic benchmark. Household needs, climate, pets and medical circumstances can require more.

How often should I check an emergency kit?

A quarterly quick check plus a deeper twice-yearly rotation is a practical system. Also review it after using supplies, moving, changing medications or adding household members or pets.

Should I buy a premade emergency kit?

A premade kit can be a starting container, but inspect every item and customize it. Household medications, food needs, documents, chargers, pet supplies and local hazards cannot be assumed.

Where should I store emergency supplies?

Use an accessible, dry location household members know. Consider dividing critical supplies so one blocked room or damaged area does not make everything unreachable.
